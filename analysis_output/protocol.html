Who Triaged You

There is one publishable paper in these three workbooks, and it is not the one I recommended yesterday. The before/after instrument comparison does not survive contact with the data — every candidate outcome is null or untestable. What does survive is sharper and more interesting: the same patient would plausibly receive a different urgency rating depending on which clinician assessed them, and that variation is measurable, replicable across two independent instruments, and not explained by physiology.

01

What I got wrong, and what replaced it

Yesterday's report called the 2023-vs-2024 instrument comparison “the most publishable thing in the three files”. I tested it. It isn't — and the correction matters enough to lead with, because building the paper around it would have produced a manuscript that dies in review.

Candidate before/after outcomeResultVerdict
Documentation completeness of the four shared vitals 97.2% → 96.1%; risk difference −1.1 pp (95% CI −2.5 to +0.4), χ² = 1.9, p = 0.167 Ceiling effect
Within-clinician change in high-acuity rate (12 raters using both forms) +2.0 pp (95% CI −16.8 to +20.9), paired t p = 0.82, Wilcoxon p = 0.42 Uninformative
Change in between-clinician variability (ICC) 0.074 → 0.113; difference +0.039 (95% CI −0.098 to +0.146), P(increase) = 0.74 Underpowered
Breadth of physiological information captured 4 items → 12 items per encounter (3.97 → 11.91 documented) True but tautological

The fourth row is the trap. The 2024 form captures airway, bilateral air entry, capillary refill, GCS, pupils, focal deficit, temperature and pain at 95–100% — and the 2023 form captures them at 0%, because the fields do not exist on it. That is a property of the form, not of clinician behaviour. It can be described in one sentence of the Discussion. It cannot be an outcome, and a reviewer will say so immediately.

There is also a hard structural ceiling: a before/after design has two units at the level of the exposure. With one pre-period and one post-period you cannot separate the instrument from everything else that changed between June 2023 and March 2025 — staffing, casemix, departmental policy, seasonality. The measured casemix drift confirms the concern rather than allaying it: respiratory rate SMD +0.232 and SpO₂ SMD −0.284 between eras, both beyond the conventional 0.10 balance threshold. No amount of regression adjustment fixes a design with two time points.

What replaced it

The rater structure that was a nuisance in the prediction framing turns out to be the substantive finding. Between-clinician variation in high-acuity designation is large, statistically unambiguous (Kruskal–Wallis H = 131.3, p = 6×10⁻¹⁸), independently replicated in the 2023 cohort under a completely different instrument (H = 75.5, p = 2.9×10⁻¹¹), and not explained by patient physiology. That is a paper.

Q6 deserves an explicit statement because it is the study everyone reaches for first. There is no clinical outcome anywhere in these files — no disposition, no admission flag, no mortality, no deterioration marker. Any model trained here predicts what a nurse wrote down, not what happened to the patient. Every claim in this protocol is written to respect that boundary.

03

The protocol

Research question

Among adult and paediatric patients triaged in a single tertiary emergency department, what proportion of the variation in high-acuity (ESI ≤ 2) designation is attributable to between-clinician differences rather than to documented patient physiology, and does a physiology-based reference model reproduce clinician assignment consistently across clinicians?

Primary hypothesis

Between-clinician variation in high-acuity designation is substantial and persists after adjustment for documented triage physiology. Formally: the between-clinician variance component τ² in a random-intercept logistic model is greater than zero, with a median odds ratio (MOR) ≥ 1.5 after adjustment. Preliminary estimate: MOR 2.04 (method-of-moments, clinicians with ≥ 20 encounters) to 2.65 (variational Bayes, all clinicians).

Secondary hypotheses
  1. H2. Documented physiology discriminates high-acuity designation with AUROC ≥ 0.70 under clinician-grouped cross-validation. Preliminary: 0.718 (LogReg) / 0.733 (HistGB).
  2. H3. Model discrimination is heterogeneous across clinicians — the per-clinician AUROC range spans more than 0.20. Preliminary: median 0.735, range 0.474–0.896.
  3. H4. Clinician-level variation replicates in the independent 2023 cohort under a different instrument (ICC > 0 with a 95% CI excluding zero). Preliminary: 0.074, CI 0.016–0.186.
  4. H5. Adding ICD-chapter features derived from free text improves discrimination by ΔAUROC ≥ 0.02 over physiology alone. Exploratory; see §6.
Objective

To quantify and partition the sources of variation in emergency triage acuity assignment, and to evaluate whether a physiology-based reference model can function as a consistency-audit tool for triage quality improvement — explicitly not as an autonomous acuity predictor.

Design

Retrospective observational analysis of a prospectively collected single-centre triage register, with a pre-specified multilevel variance-decomposition analysis, an internally and temporally validated prediction model, and an independent replication cohort. Reported per STROBE (observational) and TRIPOD+AI (prediction model).

Setting

Emergency department of a single tertiary-care hospital. Triage assessments recorded electronically at the point of triage on a structured ABCDE instrument.

Population

All consecutive triage encounters recorded on the structured instrument between December 2024 and August 2025 (n = 1,125). Replication cohort: all encounters on the preceding instrument, June 2023 – August 2024 (n = 1,311).

Inclusion
  • Triage encounter recorded on the index instrument within the study window
  • A recorded ESI category of 1–5 (primary cohort) or red-flag designation (replication cohort)
  • At least one documented vital sign
Exclusion
  • Missing or uninterpretable acuity designation (n = 3 in the primary cohort)
  • Test or template rows — e.g. the 13 records carrying the literal string Option 1
  • Encounters by clinicians contributing < 20 encounters, for the variance-component analysis only; retained in all prediction analyses with a sensitivity analysis at the ≥ 10 threshold
  • Physiologically impossible values, set to missing rather than deleting the record — pre-specified ranges: HR 20–250, SBP 50–300, DBP 20–200, RR 4–60, SpO₂ 40–100, age 0–110

Do not exclude patients for missing vitals — missingness is itself informative and complete-case deletion would bias the acuity distribution.

Comparison groups

There is no exposure and no intervention. The unit of comparison is the triaging clinician, modelled as a random effect. This is a variance-partitioning study, not a between-group comparison — framing it as one is the single most likely source of an unwarranted causal claim.

Primary outcome

High-acuity designation: clinician-assigned ESI 1 or 2 versus 3, 4 or 5. Binary, 45.0% prevalence, 505 events of 1,122. Chosen over the full 5-level scale because ESI 5 contains only 14 records and because the 1–2 / 3–5 split maps onto the operational decision of resuscitation-area allocation.

This is a clinician-assigned label, not a patient outcome. Every result statement must say so.

Secondary outcomes
  • Ordinal ESI 1–5 (proportional-odds mixed model, sensitivity)
  • Per-clinician observed/expected high-acuity ratio
  • Per-clinician model discrimination (AUROC)
  • Model calibration: intercept, slope, Brier score
Predictors

Sixteen variables, all available at the moment of triage assessment: age, sex, heart rate, systolic BP, diastolic BP, respiratory rate, SpO₂, temperature, pain score (NRS), GCS total (E+V+M), shock index (HR/SBP), capillary refill > 3 s, airway not patent, focal neurological deficit, abnormal bilateral air entry, abnormal pupils.

Excluded as leakage
  • Area Triaged — assigned with the ESI, not before it. Including it inflates AUROC from 0.733 to 0.854; 126 of 129 ESI-1 patients went to Red Triage
  • All five Intervention (*) fields — record what was done in response to the assessment
  • Disposition, GRBS — post-triage, and almost entirely empty
  • The pre-computed GCS total column — 7/1,125 filled; recompute from E+V+M
  • Shock index and its components together in the same interpretable model — collinear by construction; retain SI only in the nonlinear model, and report the linear model without it
Confounders

For the variance-component analysis: patient physiology (the 16 predictors), time of day, day of week, and calendar month (case-mix and crowding proxies). Clinician role and years of experience are not available and their absence is a stated limitation — they are the most plausible explanation for the between-clinician differences and cannot be tested.

The clustering correction that everything depends on

Mean cluster size is 32.1 encounters per clinician and the ICC is 0.113, giving a design effect of 4.53. The effective sample size is roughly 248, not 1,125. Any confidence interval computed as though the 1,125 encounters were independent will be about 2.1× too narrow. This applies to the cohort description, the subgroup comparisons and the model-performance intervals alike — not only the multilevel model.

Sample size justification

  • Variance component: 33 clinicians, 22 with ≥ 20 encounters. Reliable τ² estimation conventionally wants ≥ 30 clusters; we are at the boundary, which is why the cluster bootstrap CI is pre-specified rather than the model's asymptotic SE.
  • Prediction model: 505 events, 16 predictors = 31.6 events per variable, comfortably above the EPV ≥ 10 rule. Riley's criteria for a model with C-statistic 0.72, prevalence 0.45 and 16 predictors require roughly 600–900 — satisfied at n = 1,122.
  • What we are not powered for: the before/after contrasts of §1, any three-way subgroup analysis, and detection of an ICC difference smaller than about 0.15.

The 2023 cohort enters the paper only as a replication arm for the clinician-variation finding. Even that limited use requires knowing exactly which variables mean the same thing in both eras.

Is red=1 equivalent to ESI ≤ 2? Tested, and the answer is no.

I did not assume this. Three tests, all pre-specified as the condition for pooling:

  • Prevalence. 38.5% vs 45.0% — a 6.5 pp difference. Not disqualifying alone.
  • Physiological profile of the designated-high group. SpO₂ SMD −0.279 between the two “high acuity” groups, and −0.276 between the two “lower acuity” groups. The labels sit at different points on the physiological severity axis.
  • Discriminative structure. This is where they agree: within-era Cohen's d separating high from low is +0.296 vs +0.302 for heart rate, +0.578 vs +0.419 for respiratory rate, −0.391 vs −0.511 for SpO₂, and ~0 for systolic BP in both. The same physiology drives both labels in the same direction with similar strength.

Ruling

The two labels have similar discriminative structure but different thresholds. They may therefore be used to answer the same structural question — “does clinician identity explain variation in urgency designation?” — in each cohort separately, with the results reported side by side and never pooled. They may not be treated as a single harmonised outcome, used as a common target for one model, or compared as rates. If a reviewer asks for pooling, the answer is the SpO₂ SMD of −0.279 and the 6.5 pp prevalence gap.

05

The prediction component

Scientifically defensible, with one framing condition: the model predicts a clinician's label, not a patient's condition. Every performance number below is a measure of agreement with clinical judgement, not of clinical accuracy. Framed that way it is a consistency instrument; framed any other way it is an unvalidated clinical tool and should be rejected.

Design

Target

ESI ≤ 2 vs ESI ≥ 3. n = 1,122; 505 events (45.0%)

Models
  1. Reference: clinician identity alone (33 dummies) — establishes how much of the label is the rater
  2. Primary: multivariable logistic regression, 16 triage-time predictors, restricted cubic splines on age and the continuous vitals
  3. Comparator: gradient boosting, depth 3, 250 iterations, learning rate 0.06
  4. Negative control: the same model with Area Triaged, reported only to quantify the leakage a naive analysis would introduce
Validation
  • Clinician-grouped 5-fold CV — the primary estimate. A random split leaks clinician style between train and test
  • Temporal holdout — train ≤ 18 Apr 2025 (n = 841), test after (n = 281). Only 2 test records come from clinicians unseen in training
  • Optimism-corrected apparent performance via 500 bootstrap resamples for the logistic model
Metrics

AUROC and AUPRC with bootstrap 95% CIs; sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV at a pre-specified 90%-sensitivity operating point; calibration intercept and slope with a flexible calibration curve; Brier score. Decision-curve analysis across threshold probabilities 0.1–0.6.

Interpretability

Logistic coefficients as adjusted odds ratios with CIs — the primary interpretable output. Permutation importance on the boosted model. SHAP only if the boosted model is retained as the final model and the nonlinearity is substantive; on a 16-variable tabular problem with ΔAUROC 0.015 it is decoration, and reviewers increasingly read gratuitous SHAP plots as a red flag.

The result that must not be buried

At the clinically required operating point — 90% sensitivity, because under-triage is the dangerous error — specificity collapses to 0.21, PPV is 0.48 and NPV is 0.72. A tool at that setting would flag roughly four of every five lower-acuity patients. This belongs in the Abstract, not the Limitations. It is the honest reason the paper's conclusion is “consistency audit” rather than “decision support”, and stating it plainly is what separates this manuscript from the many triage-ML papers that report AUROC 0.73 and imply deployability.

Heterogeneity of model performance across clinicians — hypothesis H3

Among the 17 clinicians with ≥ 30 encounters, per-clinician AUROC of the grouped-CV model has a median of 0.735 and a range of 0.474 to 0.896; for 2 of 17 the model performs no better than chance. This is arguably the most clinically actionable result in the study: a physiology-based reference tracks some clinicians closely and is unrelated to others' judgements. Present it as a caterpillar plot with bootstrap CIs, and resist the temptation to name the outliers as “inaccurate” — there is no ground truth to say who is right.

Complaint-text substudy

Feasible, genuinely useful, and best positioned as a secondary analysis within the main paper rather than a separate NLP paper — the corpus is too small and too abbreviation-dense to support a methods contribution.

Design

Label

ICD-10 chapter (22 classes), collapsed to the 8–10 chapters with ≥ 30 records plus “other”. Not the specific code: with 76.9% singleton strings and 1,133 candidate codes, code-level assignment cannot be validated at this sample size.

Mapping pipeline
  1. Build an abbreviation lexicon from the corpus (RTA, SOB, CVA, AKI, CKD, ACS, K/C/O…) — expansions adjudicated by a clinician, published as a supplement
  2. Normalise: lowercase, expand abbreviations, strip qualifiers, split on / and +
  3. Candidate generation against the 1,133-entry reference list: exact → token-set fuzzy (rapidfuzz) → sentence-embedding cosine similarity
  4. Assign chapter automatically where the top candidate exceeds a pre-set similarity threshold and leads the runner-up by a margin
Human review

Two clinicians independently adjudicate (a) a random 20% sample of auto-assigned mappings and (b) all below-threshold cases. Report Cohen's κ for inter-adjudicator agreement; a third adjudicator resolves disagreements. Adjudication is performed blind to ESI — non-negotiable, since knowing the acuity would contaminate the feature.

Low-confidence handling

An explicit unmappable category, retained in the model rather than dropped. Report what fraction it represents; if it exceeds ~25% the substudy is reported as descriptive only.

Leakage control
  • The lexicon and thresholds are fitted on the training folds only and applied unchanged to held-out folds
  • Adjudicators blind to ESI, to clinician identity and to model output
  • No embedding model fine-tuned on the outcome
  • Any complaint string that encodes the disposition (e.g. text naming a resuscitation-area referral) is removed before feature construction
Evaluation

ΔAUROC of physiology + chapter features vs physiology alone, with a paired bootstrap CI on the difference under the same clinician-grouped folds — not two independently reported AUROCs. Pre-specified success threshold: ΔAUROC ≥ 0.02 with a CI excluding zero. Anything smaller is reported as “no meaningful improvement”, and reported anyway.

The APACHE cohort: excluded, and exactly what would change that

The APACHE data contributes nothing to this paper. There is no patient overlap with either triage cohort (registration-number intersection = 0), so it cannot be linked; it is a different care setting; and it has no outcome variable. Including it would be padding, and a reviewer would read it as padding.

09

Paper architecture

Working title

“Whose judgement is it? Between-clinician variation in emergency triage acuity assignment and the limits of physiology-based reference models”

Alternative, more conventional: “Clinician-level variation in Emergency Severity Index assignment: a retrospective analysis of 2,436 triage encounters across two instruments”

Target venues
  • BMC Emergency Medicine, International Journal of Emergency Medicine — realistic primary targets
  • Annals of Emergency Medicine, Academic Emergency Medicine — reach, would require the outcome data the study lacks
  • AMIA Annual Symposium / MedInfo — if the consistency-audit framing is foregrounded
  • Journal of Clinical Nursing / JEN — strong fit if the clinician-variation framing leads
Abstract

Structured, 250–300 words: Background · Methods (single-centre retrospective, two cohorts, random-intercept models, grouped CV) · Results (MOR with CI, ICC both cohorts, AUROC with CI, and the 90%-sensitivity specificity of 0.21) · Conclusions (clinician identity is a major determinant of recorded acuity; physiology-based models are suited to consistency audit, not triage decision support). The Methods sentence must contain “no patient outcome data were available”.

Introduction

ESI is the dominant triage instrument worldwide; its reliability literature is largely κ studies on vignettes or small paired-rater samples. Real-world registers rarely permit clinician-level variance decomposition because clinician identity is not retained. Triage-ML papers proliferate and almost universally report discrimination against clinician labels while implying clinical validity.

Research gap

Two gaps, stated plainly. (1) Between-clinician variation in routine practice has rarely been quantified with multilevel models on operational data with clinician identifiers retained — vignette κ studies measure something different. (2) The triage-prediction literature treats clinician variability as noise to be cross-validated away rather than as the quantity of interest; reframing the model as a consistency reference is the contribution.

Methods

STROBE + TRIPOD+AI order: design and setting · participants and eligibility · variables and definitions incl. the leakage exclusion list · data sources and cleaning rules · bias · study size and EPV · quantitative variables · statistical methods (multilevel primary, prediction secondary, replication) · missing data · ethics.

Results
  1. Cohort flow and characteristics
  2. Acuity distribution and its variation across clinicians (primary)
  3. Variance decomposition: τ², ICC, MOR, unadjusted and adjusted, both estimators
  4. Case-mix-adjusted O/E funnel plot
  5. Prediction model: discrimination, calibration, operating characteristics
  6. Per-clinician model performance heterogeneity
  7. Replication in the 2023 cohort
  8. Sensitivity analyses incl. the leakage negative control
Discussion
  • An MOR of ~2 means two clinicians assessing identical patients differ roughly two-fold in the odds of a high-acuity designation
  • Replication under a different instrument argues the effect is about clinicians, not about ESI's design
  • Physiology reaches AUROC ~0.73 against clinician labels — a ceiling that reflects genuine judgement content, not model inadequacy
  • Specificity of 0.21 at 90% sensitivity is the concrete reason this cannot be decision support
  • Implication: audit and calibration feedback for triage teams; the O/E funnel plot is directly deployable for that
  • Contrast with vignette-based κ studies, which remove case-mix variation by construction
Limitations

Single centre; non-consecutive sampling; no patient outcomes; no clinician experience or role variables — the most plausible explanation for the variation and untestable here; ESI recorded post hoc in a research register rather than the live clinical system; effective sample size 248 not 1,125; 33 clusters is at the lower bound for stable variance estimation; free-text complaint not used in the primary model; the two estimators of τ² disagree.

Conclusion

Recorded triage acuity reflects both patient physiology and the clinician performing the assessment, to a degree that is clinically non-trivial and reproducible across instruments. Physiology-based models should be positioned as consistency-audit tools for triage quality improvement, and evaluated against patient outcomes before any clinical role is considered.

Publication risk assessment

Reviewer objectionRiskPre-emptive response
“You have no outcomes — you're predicting a nurse's opinion.”Near-certainConcede it in the title framing, the Abstract Methods, and the Conclusion. The study is about the opinion; that is the point. This objection is fatal only if the paper pretends otherwise
“Single centre, non-consecutive sampling — is this a real ED population?”Near-certainReport the sampling frame honestly, compare the acuity distribution with published ESI distributions, and confine all claims to this case-mix. Do not use the word “consecutive”
“33 clinicians is too few for a variance component.”LikelyCluster bootstrap CIs rather than asymptotic SEs; report both estimators and their disagreement; present the model-free O/E funnel as corroboration
“AUROC 0.73 is not competitive with published triage models.”LikelyThose models are usually trained with post-triage variables or predict admission (a real outcome). Include the Area Triaged negative control to show precisely how a 0.85 gets manufactured
“Why not a before/after evaluation of the form redesign?”LikelyReport the three null results with CIs in the supplement. Pre-empting this converts a weakness into evidence of rigour
“Variance explained by physiology is negative — error?”PossibleNon-collapsibility of the odds ratio; cite Greenland. Report τ² for both models and never frame the difference as variance explained
“Was ethics approval obtained for secondary analysis?”CertainMust be resolved before submission. Identifiable data were collected; secondary-use approval and a data-sharing statement are prerequisites, not paperwork
“Clinician experience/role is the obvious confounder and you didn't measure it.”LikelyConcede as the principal limitation. If the department can supply role and years of experience retrospectively, get it — it would materially strengthen the paper

What would substantially strengthen the study

  1. Clinician role and years of experience — likely retrievable from staff records, and turns a variance component into an explanatory model. Highest value per unit effort.
  2. Any patient outcome for the triage cohorts — even ED disposition (admitted / discharged / died) would let you ask whether high-variance clinicians over- or under-triage relative to what happened. This is the difference between a solid specialty-journal paper and a strong one.
  3. A paired-rater substudy — 100 encounters independently triaged by two clinicians would yield a direct κ and anchor the variance component against the established reliability literature. Prospective, cheap, ~2 weeks.
  4. A second site — converts single-centre into multicentre and raises the venue ceiling considerably.

The direct answer to “is this enough for a paper?”

Yes — one paper, at a specialty journal, if it is written as a clinician-variation study with a prediction component. No, if it is written as a triage-prediction paper: at AUROC 0.73 against a subjective label with no outcomes, that manuscript is indistinguishable from dozens of weaker ones and would deserve rejection. The data are sufficient for the honest version of the study and insufficient for the ambitious one, and the gap between those is entirely a matter of framing.

One rule for the whole project

The primary analysis is pre-specified in Phase 1 and executed in Phase 4. If the MOR comes back at 1.2 with a CI spanning 1, publish that — “triage acuity assignment was consistent across clinicians in this department” is a perfectly good result and the design is what makes it credible. The failure mode to guard against is discovering that the primary is null and quietly promoting a subgroup finding to headline.
